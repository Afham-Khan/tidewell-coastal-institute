The Harrow Sound salinity network

A three-month pilot for a shellfish cooperative became a permanent monitoring site, and changed when and how harvests are closed.

The problem

Harrow Sound is a shallow estuary where the Mill river meets the sea. Shellfish beds there close whenever salinity drops below a threshold, because low salinity signals a freshwater pulse that carries bacteria from upstream farmland. Until 2021 the cooperative relied on a fortnightly boat sample. A closure could therefore last ten days for a pulse that had passed in thirty hours.

What we built

Twelve conductivity and temperature sensors were moored on a transect from the river mouth to the open sound, each logging every ten minutes. Readings were relayed to a shared dashboard and, from March 2022, to a daily text message sent to the cooperative's harvest manager.

The sensors cost about 400 each, compared with roughly 2,500 for the commercial units we benchmarked. We accepted lower absolute accuracy (plus or minus 0.3 practical salinity units) in return for density: the question was where the freshwater was, not its exact concentration.

What we found

Pulses reached the shellfish beds a median of 9 hours after the river gauge peaked, and cleared within 30 hours in 80% of events. Strong spring tides cleared them faster. That gave the cooperative a defensible rule: close on a threshold crossing, reopen after 36 hours if salinity stays above it.

Outcome

Between 2022 and 2024 the average closure shortened from 9.6 days to 2.1 days. The cooperative estimates that it recovered about 14 harvest days a year. All readings are published in dataset TW-WQ-02.
