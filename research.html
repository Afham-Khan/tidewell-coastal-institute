Research

Three programmes share one observing network. Each starts from a question a coastal community has actually asked.

The observing network

Nine stations report every ten minutes by radio or cellular link. Alder Point is the hub: it carries the longest tide-gauge record and a GNSS receiver that tracks vertical land motion to within a millimetre a year.

The remaining stations are low-cost buoys and estuary moorings, each measuring water level, temperature, conductivity and turbidity.

Programmes

What we are studying

Programme 1

Sea level and land motion

Relative sea level is what a harbour experiences, but it mixes ocean change with land movement. We pair tide gauges with GNSS so each can be reported separately. Current focus: why satellite altimetry and gauges diverge near the coast.

Programme 2

Estuary water quality

Storm runoff can close a shellfish bed within hours, but monthly sampling sees none of it. Continuous sensors reveal the timing, and we link it to rainfall and tide state. Current focus: the Harrow Sound salinity network.

Programme 3

Instruments and methods

Our buoy design costs roughly a fifth of a commercial equivalent. We publish calibration procedures, drift models and failure logs so that other groups can build, test and criticise them.

Featured case study

The Harrow Sound salinity network started as a three-month pilot for a shellfish cooperative and became a permanent part of the observing network.

Methods and review

Each programme publishes a methods note before its first result. Our advisory panel reviews these annually, and the review reports appear alongside our publications.
